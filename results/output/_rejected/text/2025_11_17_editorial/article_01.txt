Why universities are boycotting global rankings: 'Black boxes, work as closed systems'

e EDUCATION

Abhinaya Harigovind New Delhi, November 16

IN SEPTEMBER, Sorbonne University, located in Paris, announced its withdrawal from the ranking produced by Times Higher Education (THE), becoming yet another major institution to flag concerns about international higher education rankings. Widely publicised and closely watched, global higher education rankings produced by different organisations, analysts or platforms, can determine perceptions among prospective students and employers, and drive policy decisions. The rankings have, however, drawn flak over the years. Key concerns among institutions, including some Indian ones, have been that the rankingsare not fully transparent in terms of the datathey consider, and that ascore and rank do not capture the extent and diversity of activities, contributions, and research. How international rankings work There are three prominent global higher education rankings: the QS rankings produced by London-based Quacquarelli Symonds; the THE rankings, produced by THE magazine, also headquartered in London; and rankings produced by the ShanghaiRanking Consultancy. For THE, institutions submit data used for the rankings, which allows them to stop participating in the process by withholding these submissions. In contrast, QS and ShanghaiRanking use public data.

Sorbonne University's concerns The institution - ranked 72 in the 2026 QS ranking, 76 in the 2026 THE ranking, and 43 in the 2025 Shanghai Ranking - flagged concerns about indicators that measure reputation and research. "Their (global rankings') methodology, which focuses on English-language journals, favours certain disciplines, while the humanities and social sciences... are put at a disadvantage... Certain rankings are based on reputational surveys, which raise numerous concerns, including scientific, methodological, and ethical issues," Sorbonne University told The Indian Express.
