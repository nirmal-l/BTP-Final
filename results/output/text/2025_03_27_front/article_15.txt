'Thad only Rs 100": In Surat labour colony, memories of long walk home 5 yrs ago

HUDDLED ON mats laid over concrete lanes that still bear faint traces of gulaal from the recent Holi festivities, groups of women and girls glue sequins on shararas and kurtas in the bylanes of Bapunagar, a labour colony in Surat's Pandesara industrial area.

Five years ago, these lanes, home totrained migrant workers who form the backboneof Surat's

dyeing, printing, powerloom and embroidery units, were nearly empty as many of them had decided to walk hundreds of kilometres back to their homes. It was a walk like none other. On March 24, 2020, as the government announced a sudden lockdown - suspending ali modes of transport and urging people to stay indoors - to check the spread of the rampaging Covid-19 virus, at labour hubs across the country, from Surat to Kolkata, Chennai to Gurgaon, migrant workers trudged hundreds of kilometres back to their homes in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh and Rajasthan.

Among those who walked then was Ramanand Paswan, 35, atextile factory worker in Surat. Around three months after the lockdown, with no workand his savings drying up, Ramanand had decided to go back to his family in Gongara, a village in Bihar's Aurangabad district nearly 2,000 km from Surat. Five years later, now back where he began that night, he says, "We were 10 ofus, ali from different villages in Aurangabad. People were dying all around us and I thought, if I have to die, I would rather die at home, with my family around me. When finally left, had Rs 100 in my pocket. Icarried a water bottle, a

few packets of biscuits, my mobile phone and charger, and three pairs of clothes." With a nationwide curfew in place, Ramanand and his friends decided to walk at night, when the police weren't looking - or when they looked away. From Surat, the group walked to Nandurbar in Maharashtra, then crossed over into Madhya Pradesh. "Somewhere at the Maharashtra-Madhya Pradesh border, we stopped a truck and got a ride for 10 kilometres. After that, we continued our walk. We would start walking early in the morning, stop at noon, rest tillthe sun went down, and start walking again. Itwaseasier to walk at

night. Sometimes, we would stop at one of the villages and if we were lucky, we would get some food, There were many nights and days when we only had water," he says, sitting on a plastic chair in Bapunagar. Hedoesn't remember the number of days he walked - "over a month, I think". After two years at home, by the end of 2022, Ramanand returned to Surat - back to the factory in Pandesara, which he joined eight yearsago, the flatin Bapunagar and even the same roommates, Six months ago, he moved his wife Sandhya, 32, and their two children to Surat. "The children study at a local municipal

school. Sandhya also makes some money sticking sequins on readymade clothes. Unlike Surat, we haveno other means of livelihood back home in Bihar. This is why] decided to shift my family here," says Ramanand, who earns Rs 20,000 a month. Mostresidents of Bapunagar work in units in the Pandesara and Sachin areas of Surat, which is among the largest textile producersin the country.
