Little Foot tells a big evolution story

40 YEARS AGO December 19,1985

NEW study on "Little Foot", one of the world's most complete early human fossils, could reshape the understanding of human evolution as it stands. First discovered in 1994-1998 in the cave system of Sterk-fontein, South Africa, and unveiled to the public in 2017, "Little Foot" was long thought to be either a member of the Australopithecus prometheus or of the Australopithecus africanus. The new study, published in the American Journal of Biological Anthropology, posits that the skeleton belongs to neither family. It may actually be part of anew species of human ancestorsentirely. This is the latest addition to the ever-changing picture of evolution. Initially thought to be linear, evolution is increasingly being seen as a more diffuse process. Just as the discoveries with "Little Foot" point to the existence of another branch in the human familytree, another discovery earlier this year challenges the very timeline of human evolution. A skull discovered by a team of researchers in China was thought to belong to the Homo erectus, given that it was a million years old. However, genetic studies on the remnant uncovered that it likely belonged to Homo longi, aspecies with similar levels of cerebral development as Homo sapiens and Neanderthals. That theevolution of Homosapniens - in fact, thespecies' very existence - has been shaped by more factors than previously imagined makes for a richer story, one that should deepen humans' appreciation for theirown ability to adapt andsurvive. In atime ofgreat existential threats - climate change, wars, AI - this ever-complicating picture of evolution offers both solace and upliftment. There may be much that humans areupagainst - but prehistory shows that they are up to the challenge.
