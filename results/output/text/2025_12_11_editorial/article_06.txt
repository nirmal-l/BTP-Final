HAT DO you do with privilege in a society as deeply unequal and stratified as ours? The easiest, most self-serving worldview to fall back on,to be virtuous without confronting guilt, is summed up by the advice Nick Carraway gets from his fatherin The Great Gatsby: "Whenever you feel like criticising any one... just remember that all the people in this world haven't had theadvantages that you vehad." Butis itenoughjust toacknowledge your own luck? Orcan weuse it togo beyond the cocoon of comfort? There iscurrently in Delhian exhibition - eight paintings - titled 'Nothing Human is Alien to Me'. The artist, Aban Raza (full disclosure, an old acquaintance), is from Delhi. She is English-speaking and, inthelarger scheme of things, on the upper endof oursociety. Her works, though, look outward: At victims ofriots, working-class women, workers engaged in a tragic protest. For me, the chance visit onSaturday afternoon was arevelation. Not because her subjects were alien, but because they were, despite not being so in every social and economic indicator that academics, analysts and journalists love so much, "just like us". Itis in that sentiment, one that isall too rare in howwe see the world and eachother, that the boundariesof moraluniverses are drawi. Can a privileged person talk about the suffering of others? A tich person ofand forthe poor;a Brahmin man fora marginalised woman? Over the past few decades, the issue has become complicated. For centuries, victims of the "system" - whether capitalism, caste, class, colonialism, racism, or majoritarianism - wereunable to tell their own stories or, more often, were unheard even when they shouted from the rooftops. And the contours of structural oppressionextend culpability notjustasa matterofinexploitation. For those ontop to tell the stories that are not their own, then, is "appropriation" and self-serving grandstanding. As more people whowere silenced are finally heard, albeit in aproportion far short of what would be just and right, itis fair for those who havespokenall this while tobesilent, nottopontificate on suffering they do not and cannot know. Unfortunately, such a position, while well-meaning, can also be self-defeating. At atime when social media encourages competing victim-hoods by saying that only experience and identity can form the basis of morality and ethics, we lose out on the larger idea of the universal. Meanwhile, theconservative, the revanchist, the ideology offering "civilisational values" can double downand grow in popularity. Across the world, minorities and migrants are being vilified, and gains made onrights and decency are in retreat. The answer is not ignoring identity, privilege or decency. It's noteven in politics aswe know it It is in acknowledging, as only art can, that empathy is an absolute virtue - thesuffering of the unemployed and the man who is beaten, while the police look on, can easily be that of any of us. That "nothing human is alien tome". But then today, it may be that a positive, humanist universalism is only possible in aset of paintings.
