INJUSTICE UNCHECKED

SC spoke strongly and firmly against arbitrary bulldozer action by state governments. It needs to make itself heard

N MONDAY, THE Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation sent a team to the hotel that houses the studio where Kunal Kamra shot his now-controversial stand-up comedy show in which he tooka jibe at Maharashtra's Deputy Chief Minister and, for that, was targeted bya mob - the BMC team carried out an inspection of the site and got an "unauthorised" shed pulled down. On Monday, too, the Supreme Court issued a notice to the Malvan Municipal Council for demolishing the shop and house of the family of a 14-year-old who had allegedly chanted "anti-India" slogans during the India-Pakistan Champions Trophy cricket match in February. The same day, the Nagpur bench of the Bombay High Court stayed the demolition of houses of the accused in the recent violence in the city - but the court order came too late to prevent the razing of the home of the key accused. It's not just Maharashtra. A rampant bulldozer injustice, which unabashedly flouts due process and selectively and disproportionately targets the minority and the poor, in which demolitions are threatened and carried out by state governments of homes and properties of people accused in crimes in the guise of acting against "illegal encroachment", is spreading across states. In Gujarat, it is part of what the police describe as a"first of its kind whole-of-govermment approach", which also includes parading the accused in public and cancelling bail. The template, of course, was laid down in UttarPradesh, where it has earned the moniker of "Bulldozer Baba" for Chief Minister Yogi Adityanath. The rash of arbitrary demolitions, across states - in Punjab, the AAP's Bhagwant Mann government may be the latest totake a leafout of the BJP CM's playbook - flout the strong and clear directions laid down by the country's apex court. In September last year, the Supreme Court took up the matter and in November it laid down pan-India guidelines on bulldozer action, putting up guardrails, streamlining the procedure for identifying unauthorised structures, issuing notice to persons concerned, giving them a fair hearing and subsequent action. In the way in which they were being carried out, the court said, the demolitions breached principlesof natural justice and separation of powers, violated the citizen's right toshelter and inflicted collective punishment on the family of the accused. Action against the accused, or even against convicts, without following due process was against the constitutional ethos, it said. The Court's intervention was reassuring, even if belated. But it also gave rise to acrucial question - now that the Court had taken a strong position, how would itensure that it was respected, and that its authority was not undermined? After all, even on hate speech, the SC had in April 2023 directed states to register cases on their own, without waiting for complaints. Any hesitation to doso, it had said, would be viewed as contempt of court. There is little evidence, however, that the Court's firmness has had an appreciable effect so far. That, then, is the Court's burden and challenge on bulldozer injustice as well. The SC needs to follow up, and mechanisms need tobe put in place that nudge state governments and lower courts to abide by what it has laid down, in letter and spirit.

While the landscape of research has expanded, doctors in large parts ofthe Global South, including India, continue to rely on the sputum test. The anti-TB programme's plan to widen the diagnostic net by using other samples - such as blood, saliva, or stool - is in line with the WHO's new guideline to "invest in novel diagnostic techniques". The new initiative seems to have accounted for the longstanding weakness of the Indian healthcare system - the shortage of facilities and trained professionals in rural areas. The use of Al to read microscopy slides and X-rays could be a breakthrough. Untilrecently, the standard medication course took six to nine months to complete, and treatment for drug-resistant tuberculosis could take up to two years with much lower chances of cure. In the past five years, new drugs have helped reduce the length of therapy. Even then, the regimen is taxingon patients and their caregivers, The defaulting patients then run the risk of contracting the more virulent multidrug-resistant version of the disease and spreading it. A simple test can now measure compliance with the drug regimen - whena patient's salivais put on the strip, itcan tellwhetherthey have taken the medicine in thelast 24 hours. The government would, however, be doing injustice to its initiative by relying solely on technology.
