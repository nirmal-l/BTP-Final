BUYING IO BE SEEN

The rise of influencer culture has redefined what it means to belong

Instagram has made consumption feel intimate. We don't just buy products but the fantasy of being someone slightly better than who we are. We aren't simply choosing a thing - we're selecting a tribe, amood board, a way to signal to the world, this is me. But what if belonging wasn't for sale?

Sociologist Thorstein Veblen had a name forthis in the 19th century: "conspicuousconsumption." But today's version is more slippery. It's not just about faunting weaitii - it's about cloaking consumption in relatability. It's the illusion that thisisn't excess; it's empowerment. The influencer with perfect skin isn't selling moisturiser - she's selling the belief that you, too, could look that way, if only you tried (and bought) hard enough. The market has responded accordingly. Influencer marketing is now a multi-billion-pound industry, built on trust, aesthetics, and psychology. A McKinsey study reports that nearly 75 per cent of Gen Z consumers are guided by social media. What usedtobecommerceisnowcontent. The Shop tab is ashortcut todesire. The algorithm learns your wants before youname them. But behind the soft filters lies a sharper truth: The entire architecture of Instagram relies on us not feeling enough. Its most powerful currency is our quiet dissatisfaction. If we were content, we wouldn't scroll. Ifwe weren't scrolling, we wouldn't buy. This is the quiet violence of it.
