The sidelining of the perspective of indigenous people, despite their hard labour in mothering Al, is what shapes Sahay's intricate narrative. The silencing of one narrative in favour of another to create a dominant Al model is nothing but reinforcement of Western hegemony that women like Nehma challenge. The symbiotic relationship between indigenous communities and nature, where even a porcupine is shown guiding Nehma's daughter when she is lost in the forest, defies the modern understanding that sees the world in terms of resources. The binary of useful and harmful is turned on its head. Sahay shows that when Al is taught a non-mono-lithicepistemic structure, it leams nuances of the marginalised, instead of mathematical categorisation. Humans in the Loop opens up possibilities to revolutionise Al's worldviews. When Nehma's boss asks Al to show images of Indian tribal women, it produces pictures of white women with some markers of "perceived" indigenous cultures - like a romanticised headgear or a flower stuck in the hair. They don't represent the realities of Nehmaandothers like her. But asa mother and

atrainer, Nehma knows the way out. She feeds her ownimages, clicked by her daughter, into the machine. In the next search, the technology shrugs offits inherent bias anddelivers an image that is closer to the reality of "tribal woman in Jharkhand". Nehma's boss then exclaims, "Al is like a child..It can change. It just needs time and a whole lot of data." Beyond this schema of preferential data feeding and competitive perceptions lies the mother in Nehma, who chooses to "mother" an Al so that she may be her own children's legally recognised mother. The blurring of these lines between Al-human, human-nature andnature-Al is what makes Sahay's film amasterpiece. Its use of silence, the absence of Savarna romanticism and lack of melodrama keep the audience focused on Nehma's resilience and innovation.
