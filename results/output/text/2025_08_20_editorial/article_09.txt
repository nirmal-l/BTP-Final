The 1990s and early 2000s were obsessed with consumerism, status, excess. Today, however, our closets are increasingly burdened with questions: Who madethis garment? At what cost to the planet? In AJLT, these questions bubble to the surface. Che Diaz (Sara Ramirez), a non-binary stand-up comic, introduces a wardrobe that challenges the rigid gender dichotomyof fashion. Thisis a departure fromthe worldof SATC, where femininity was performed through heels and handbags, Charlotte's daughter Lily experiments with identity, prompting conversations about how Gen Z and Gen Alpha navigate fashion. Together, these characters expand the canvas of fashion. Carrie trades her sky-high heeis for practical footwear in certain episodes, because age and experience demand new definitions of comfort and elegance. Perhaps the most meaningful shift lies in what fashion no longer hides. In SATC, clothes werea shield - Carrie wore coutureeven when her bank account was near empty; Miranda suited up to hide vulnerability. In AJLT, fashion is still powerful, but it is porous. It reveals inse-Curities, contradictions, and the discomfort of ageing inaculture that worships youth. Carrie's

layered, sometimes eccentric outfits reflect a Wolnan renegotiating her sense of selfafter loss. Charlotte's polished looks clash with her messy attempts to be the "perfect" mother. Fashion is no longer fantasy alone; itis friction. Itis this push-and-pull that makes AjJLT worth taking seriously. It doesn't abandon the joy of dressing up. Butit tempers this joy with honesty. The show's styling reflects a world where fashion is not about perfection but about negotiation. Critics often complain that AJLT lacks the sparkle of the original, But that lack of sparkle is in itself the point. Fashion in 2025 cannot dazzle us like in 1998. We are no longer innocent consumers. We know the labour conditions behind fast fashion, the environmental cost of discarded textiles, and the hollowness ofendless consumption. Together, the two shows form a continuum: One taught us fashion could be joy, and the other reminds us it must also be responsibility. It doesn't lose relevance with age or social change; it simply asks harder questions. And in those questions, fashion remains what it has always been: A mirror to who we are, and who we dare to become.
