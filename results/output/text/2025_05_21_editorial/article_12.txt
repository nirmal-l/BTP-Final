Now Trump says a peace deal would have to be negotiated directly between Russia and Ukraine. But core obstacles remain AST YEAR, US President Donald Trump claimed he would end the warin Ukraine within "24 hours". More recently, he suggested that the conflict could only be resolved through an in-person meeting between himself and Russian President Vladimir Putin. Following a two-hour phone call with Putin on Monday, Trump said that any peace agreement would have to be negotiated directly between Russia and Ukraine. This sudden pivot away from his earlier stance underscores the complex nature of the ongoing deadlock between Ukraine and Russia. Evenas Trump posted on social media that Kyiv and Moscow would "immediately start" negotiations fora ceasefire, Putin rejected a US-proposed 30-day unconditional ceasefire - one Ukraine has already agreed to. The Russian President has consistently rejected such proposals, arguing that a temporary halt would allow Ukraine timeto regroup and rearm. He instead reiterated on Monday Moscow's demand for addressing what he calls the "root causes" of the conflict. In February, Trump had announced that peace talks would begin "immediately". Back then, however, Kyiv was not invited tothe table, prompting Furopean leaders to rally around Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. Today, both Trump and Zelenskyy appear to have moved on from their infamous televised spat in late February, andthey recently signed the much-touted critical minerals deal. Washington is now making efforts to bring both Kyiv and Moscow into direct negotiations. Yet, the core obstacles to peace remain unchanged: Russia currently occupies about afifth of Ukraine and insists that Kyiv cedethis land, which would eventually be rendered permanently "neutral". Ukraine insists on a full Russian withdrawal and seeks NATO membership as a long-term security guarantee. There is no quick fix. Less than aweek ago, Russia and Ukraine held their first direct peace talks (proposed by Putin) since 2022 in Istanbul. While they failed to make meaningful progress on the territory issue, the two delegations agreed to exchange 1,000 prisoners of war each. A Ukrainian delegate reportedly described Moscow's demands as "detached from reality". Still, the mere fact that these direct talks took place is significant. The next crucial step must be securing an immediate ceasefire - creating the space needed to tackle the more difficult questions suriGunding a lasting peaceagreement. Putin reportedly declined to attend the Istanbul meeting despite being invited by Zelenskyy. For a way out, leadership mustrise above optics and meaningful engagement must be prioritised.
