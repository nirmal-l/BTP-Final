DELHI'S MUCH-ANTICIPATED cloud-seeding experiment on October 28 belied the hope that artificial showers would wash away the thick haze that has been choking the city since Diwali. While disappointment over the failure is understandable, this outcome should not come as a surprise. It rein-forces what environmental scientists have long maintained: The capital's pollution problem cannot be dispersed with one-off, high-visibility interventions. Each year, as autumn fades into winter, Delhi's skyline turns into a haze of toxic grey. With air quality indices breaching hazardous levels, the pressure to act swiftly intensifies. The intent behind the latest response - a pilot cloud-seeding experiment aimed at inducing rainfall that can temporarily wash pollutants from the atmosphere - is understandable. However, it is critical to ask whether the intervention can effectively address Delhi's deeply entrenched and complex air pollution problem. Cloud seeding is a weather modification technique thatinvoives dispersing substances such as silver iodide or sodium chloride into clouds to encourage rainfall. It has been usedin several countries, including China, theUS, and the UAE, mostly to tackle drought or enhance water availability. In India, it has been used intermittently in states such as Maharashtraand Karnataka for similar purposes. Its use to reduce air pollution, however, remains rare and scientifically incondusive. Even globally, cloud seeding hasoffered localised andtemporaryimprovementin air quality. Delhi's pollution pattern is not a mystery. Studies by IT Delhi, TERI, and the System of Air Quality and Weather Forecasting and Research (SAFAR) have shown that air quality deteriorates sharply in October and November due to a combination of anthropogenic and meteorological factors. The science of Delhi's pollution is clear. Yet, policy responses often take the form of short-term or symbolic measures. Air pollution, however, is a regional phenomenon influenced by emissions from across the National Capital Region(NCR)and neighbouring states - localised rain over a few square km cannot solve aregional crisis. Air pollution is tied to our patterns of energy use, transport systems, waste management, and agricultural practices. Short-term "visibility" actions often divert resources and attention from more meaningful interventions such as strict enforcement of emission norms, transition to cleaner fuels and technologies, improved public transport, improving waste management, and better coordination between Delhi and neighbouring states onissues such ascrop residue buming and industrial emissions. WONDER IS IN REAL WORLD

In the age of Al-generated videos, to keep the joy of authentic discovery alive, people must look elsewhere NCE, THERE WAS fame. Then, the internet happened, and fame's cousin, virality, entered the chat. Behind fameis a face, behind virality, a moment. () Whether caught, captured or created on camera, a moment - free of the burden of a person and a personality - can drive views, profit, influence. Ifyou can create it, youcan mine it for all its worth - social media influencers, corporate sales teams and IT departments of political parties all have figured this out. This is why, as Al tools become handy, doctored and/or Al-created videos have been gaining ground. Recently, a video of adrunkman petting a tiger and offering it alcohol in Madhya Pradesh went viral. Incomments sections and online articles, people expressed awe and suspicion: A moving scene caught on screen or a farce created by Al? Only long after its wide circulation did the fact that it was the latter come to light. This series of events has now become par for the course. Today, on the intemet, the fake sits right next to the authentic. Telling the two apart is near impossible - and nobody wants to be fooled. The result? Netizens who have turned into citizen detectives. Every viral video, every piece of music, art, or writing is met with suspicion. The joy of being able to relate to people across oceans, the excitement of discovering new talent, new art, and the awe at the universe revealing itself are all marred by asense of distrust. Indiscovering how to crack the algorithm, then, whatis lost is more than just authenticity: It is alsothe sense of wonder which the promise of authenticity once offered. After all, who doesn't want adrunk man and a lonely tiger to be friends? Maybe then, what is needed istolook elsewhere. Step away from theintemet and into the real world: Wonders still abound.
