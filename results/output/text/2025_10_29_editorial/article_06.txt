THE ASIAN PACTS

America's need to counter China's control over critical mineral supply chains is writ large on US deals in Asia-Pacific FTER MONTHS OF tariff threats and trade disputes, countries across Southeast Asiaare seeking to stabilise economic ties with the United States, A amajor export market for the region. For Vietnam, the Philippines, Thailand, and Cambodia - the countries affected by 19-20 per cent tariffs imposed by the Donald Trump administration earlier this year - the US is among the top export destinations. Southeast Asia is also a crucial market for American products. The US is also looking to secure greater accessto global supply chains, particularly for critical minerals. US President Donald Trump is in Asia this week on his first visit to the region since 2017, with stops in Malaysia, Japan, and South Korea. A possible meeting with Chinese President Xi Jinping is also on the cards, though Beijing has yet to confirm this, Trump's first stop was Kuala Lumpur, where he attended the ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations) summit. US imports from ASEAN reached $352.1 billion in 2024, up from around $140 billion in 2017. On Sunday, Trump signed trade and critical minerals agreements with Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, and Vietnam, though without any reduction in existing tariffs. These deals, however, eliminate several tariff and non-tariff barriers on US exports to the region. Meanwhile, China announced on Tuesday an upgrade to its free trade agreement with ASEAN (their bilateral trade totalled $771 billion last year). After presiding over the signing of a peace deal between Thailand and Cambodia, Trump travelled to Japan, where he signed an agreement with the country's newly elected first woman Prime Minister, Sanae Takaichi, that focused on "securing the supply of critical minerals and rare earths through mining and processing". This follows China's recent tightening of export controls onrare earths, which has given Beijing significant leverage in global supply chains. The agreements signed in Malaysia and Japan are part of Washington's strategy to diversify its sources of critical minerals, Trump is expected to meet Xi Jinping on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC)summitin Gyeongju, South Korea, on Thursday. Preparations for this meeting have been underway; on Saturday, American and Chinese officials met in Kuala Lumpur for preliminary discussions. These talks reportedly sought to chart a path forward in the ongoing trade war, evenas Trump threatened to impose new 100 per cent tariffs on Chinese goods starting November 1, in retaliation for Beijing's rare earths export controls, Should there be a détente in the trade war, India will need to swiftly recalibrate its China strategy. Regardless of what happens between Trump and Xi, New Delhineeds to mend fences with Washington.
